# My Openings

Small repertoire, deeply known. That's deliberate — I'd rather know six moves
cold than twenty vaguely. But see the warning at the bottom.

> **CONFIRM:** the gambit answers below are standard theory and fit my setup,
> but check them against what my coach actually told me and adjust.

---

## As White — 1.e4, Italian setup

**The move order:**

1. `e4`
2. `Nf3` (attacking e5)
3. `Bc4` (aiming at f7 — the weakest square in Black's camp)
4. `d3` (solid, supports e4, opens the c1 bishop)
5. `Nc3`
6. `O-O` — **by move 10 at the latest.** In Game 3 I castled on move 16 and it
   was too late.

**The idea:** fast, safe development. Both bishops out, both knights out, king
safe. I'm not trying to win in the opening — I'm trying to arrive at a
middlegame with everything working.

**The danger:** this is exactly where Leak #2 lives. The moment the opponent
does something unusual, the script stops and Scan A starts.

## As Black — 1...e5

Mirror logic against 1.e4.

1. `...e5`
2. `...Nc6`
3. `...Bc5` or `...Nf6`
4. `...d6`
5. `...O-O` — **by move 10.**

**Four Knights Game: Italian Variation** — this arises constantly from my move
orders (both colours). Lichess specifically recommended it as my puzzle theme
after Game 8, which means it's where my real positions come from. Worth a
dedicated puzzle set.

## How to meet the gambits I actually face

### Scholar's Mate attempt (early `Qh5` or `Qf3` + `Bc4`)

The threat is mate on f7. It is not a real attack, but it must be answered.

- Against **`Qh5`**: play **`...g6`**, hitting the queen and defending f7. If
  the queen then takes on e5+, block with `...Qe7` and I come out ahead — the
  queen has to run and I develop with tempo.
- Against **`Qf3`**: play **`...Nf6`**, developing AND covering the mate.
- **Never** panic-defend with `...Qe7` blocking my own bishop if a developing
  move does the job.

**Key point:** an early queen sortie is the exact "unusual move" trigger from
Scan B. Every tempo I gain kicking that queen around is free.

### Evans Gambit (`1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.b4`)

White offers the b4 pawn to gain time and build a big centre with `c3` and `d4`.

**Simplest practical answer — accept, then give it back:**

1. `4...Bxb4` (take it)
2. `5.c3 Ba5` (retreat, keep the pin on the c3 pawn)
3. `6.d4 d6` (solid — do NOT get greedy trying to hold everything)
4. Develop normally, aim to trade pieces. **The gambit's whole value is
   initiative; every trade kills it.**

**If declining feels safer:** `4...Bb6` is fine and completely playable. I lose
nothing by declining a gambit I don't know.

### General rule for ANY gambit

I do not need to refute it. I need to:

1. Take the pawn once if it's safe.
2. **Give it back** the moment holding it costs me development.
3. Finish developing, castle, and trade pieces.

A gambit that gets traded down is just a pawn down.

## The honest caveat

My opening is my most-scripted phase and therefore my most dangerous. The
repertoire is not the problem — **playing it without looking is**. Every move
in this file is subject to Scan A. Theory tells me what's normal; the scan
tells me what's actually on the board.
